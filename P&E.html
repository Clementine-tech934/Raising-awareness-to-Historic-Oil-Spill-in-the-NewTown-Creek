From Natural Marshland to Industrial Drain

Long before factories lined its shores, Newtown Creek was a thriving tidal wetland system filled with clean water, marshes, and abundant shellfish. During the 19th century, the American Industrial Revolution changed this landscape completely. More than fifty oil refineries, petrochemical plants, metal-plating factories, and fertilizer works popped up along its banks, treating the waterway as a convenient and unregulated dumping ground for toxic byproducts.

The Legacy of Oil Spills and Black Mayonnaise

Decades of unchecked industrial activity culminated in catastrophic underground petroleum leaks, most notably the massive Greenpoint oil spill, which leaked an estimated 17 to 30 million gallons of oil into the local soil and water table. At the bottom of the creek, more than twenty feet of "black mayonnaise" has accumulated—a toxic, oxygen-depleted sludge composed of heavy metals, chemical residues, and old debris. Because the natural freshwater streams were paved over or filled in, the creek behaves like a stagnant inlet where historical pollutants remain trapped.

Sewage Overflows and Ecosystem Impact

Compounding the industrial legacy, New York City’s antiquated sewer infrastructure relies on combined sewer overflows, which discharge untreated raw sewage and street runoff directly into the creek during heavy rains. Despite this harsh environment, resilient local wildlife like wading birds and fish still attempt to use the habitat, though they face severe health risks from toxins like PCBs and heavy metals.
