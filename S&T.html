The Early Industrial Roots

Newtown Creek began its transformation from a natural tidal estuary into an industrial corridor during the mid-nineteenth century. The establishment of the country's first kerosene refinery in 1854 and subsequent modern oil facilities turned the Greenpoint shoreline into a dense network of refineries, storage tanks, and pipelines operated by corporate entities like Standard Oil. Decades of unmitigated spills, routine operational leaks, and the direct discharge of industrial waste went largely unregulated as the local economy boomed.

Hidden Dangers and Discovery

Warning signs bubbled under the surface for years, most dramatically on October 5, 1950, when a massive underground gasoline explosion at Huron Street and Manhattan Avenue shot 25 manhole covers three stories into the air. The true scale of the ecological disaster finally came to light on September 2, 1978, when a routine U.S. Coast Guard helicopter patrol spotted an iridescent plume of oil pouring directly into Newtown Creek near Meeker Avenue. Subsequent testing revealed an enormous underground free-product petroleum plume spanning over 52 acres beneath the neighborhood.

Accountability and Cleanup

Public awareness and legal pressure grew significantly when local watchdogs and environmental groups like Riverkeeper pushed for accountability and remediation. Activists and state agencies filed major federal lawsuits against corporate successors like ExxonMobil, leading to initial containment and recovery operations. In 2010, the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency officially designated Newtown Creek a federal Superfund site, initiating ongoing multi-decade cleanup and ecological restoration efforts that continue today.
